## Abstract (≤250 words)

Recent network-scale studies report that earthquakes leave growth signatures in tree-ring width series hundreds of kilometres from the rupture, raising the prospect of reconstructing multi-century earthquake histories from existing tree-ring archives. We test this prospect in Japan with a pre-registered analysis of all 13 International Tree-Ring Data Bank (ITRDB) ring-width sites in the country (650 series, 1–2005 CE, including a merged Yakushima record with ≥10 living trees continuously since 644 CE) against the USGS and historical earthquake catalogues. Before searching for signals, we calibrated the detection statistic by injecting earthquake-shaped growth suppressions into the real series: this quantified how much of the signal each standardisation method preserves (0.93–1.02 for five methods), the sensitivity of each site (1.8–3.2 z-score units per unit suppression amplitude), and hence the amplitude at which a real event could have gone unnoticed. Across 3,193 site–event pairs (M ≥ 6.5) the detection rate was 6.1 %, close to the 5 % false-positive rate of the test, a physical ground-motion prior had no skill in predicting which pairs were detected (AUC 0.46), and superposed epoch analyses were non-significant at every site, in pooled form, and for maximum latewood density. The 95 % upper bound on stand-mean growth loss is 7.1 % for the 1952 Tokachi-oki (M8.1) and 9.3 % for the 1941 Hyuga-nada (M8.0) earthquakes, robust to timing, geometry, stacking and standardisation choices. Applying published “responsive-tree” criteria to random years reproduces the fractions reported as earthquake responses, and a year-shift null that preserves the spatial co-occurrence of events removes the significance of a marginal resistance decrease (p = 0.005 → 0.03–0.05); the pre-specified primary test gives p = 0.07 and a pooled bound of 4.7 %. Detecting a 10 % far-field signal at 3σ would require ~140 sites of 30 trees; near-fault sampling remains necessary for dendroseismic reconstruction.

**Keywords:** dendroseismology; superposed epoch analysis; detection limit; injection test; Japan; ITRDB

## 1. Introduction

Trees growing on or next to a fault rupture can record the event in their rings. Along the San Andreas fault, trees split or tilted by the 1812 and 1857 earthquakes show abrupt, multi-year growth suppression that dates the events to the year (Jacoby et al., 1988; Sheppard and Jacoby, 1989); the 1943 Tosya earthquake on the North Anatolian fault left a linear trail of suppressed trees along the surface rupture (Kozacı, 2012); and the death year of drowned cedars, combined with a Japanese tsunami record, fixed the 1700 Cascadia earthquake to a single winter (Yamaguchi et al., 1997; Jacoby et al., 1997). In all these cases the trees were within metres to a few kilometres of the rupture, and the mechanism—root shearing, tilting, subsidence, or inundation—was physically obvious (Jacoby, 1997). A recent survey of tree damage in strong-motion records concludes that only very strong shaking breaks a tree (Hough, 2026); the same question for growth, rather than breakage, has not been asked quantitatively.

A different and more ambitious use of tree rings has emerged in the last decade. Rather than sampling near-fault survivors, several studies analyse existing regional networks of climate-oriented tree-ring sites, mostly drawn from the International Tree-Ring Data Bank (ITRDB), and report earthquake signatures far from the source. In the Pamir, growth suppressions attributed to earthquakes were identified up to 230 km from epicentres (Owczarek et al., 2017). In the Himalayas, 64–77 % of trees at 46 sites were classified as responsive to five historical earthquakes, with growth declines lasting one to five years and a dependence on epicentral distance out to about 200 km (Pandey et al., 2023). At the global scale, Gao et al. (2024) linked ITRDB ring-width series to the post-1900 USGS catalogue at sites that experienced Modified Mercalli intensity ≥ 4, tested growth changes with a double-bootstrap superposed epoch analysis (SEA; Rao et al., 2019), and reported decade-scale shifts in forest resilience after earthquakes in seven regions. If far-field growth signals of this kind are real and separable from climate, the ITRDB—with thousands of multi-century chronologies—becomes a palaeoseismic archive, and the temporal clustering of large earthquakes over many centuries could be read from trees rather than from trenches.

Two properties of that literature motivate the present study. First, the detection statistics were not calibrated: none of the studies quantified how large a growth suppression the method would have to see in order to declare a response, nor what fraction of “responsive” trees the same criteria produce in years without earthquakes. Second, significance was assessed against nulls in which event years are drawn independently for each site, although large earthquakes are shared by many sites in the same year and regional climate is shared as well. Both properties can inflate the apparent evidence, and both can be checked directly.

Japan offers an unusually clean test case. It has a dense instrumental catalogue since 1900 with several M ≥ 7.5 subduction earthquakes whose shaking at inland forests reached Japan Meteorological Agency intensity 5 to 6 (peak ground accelerations of 100–250 gal), a well-documented historical catalogue back to the seventh century, and a small but long ITRDB network that includes a Yakushima *Cryptomeria japonica* record beginning in the first century CE. Japan was not among the regions analysed by Gao et al. (2024), so it is an out-of-sample test of the far-field premise with the same data sources and the same statistical tools.

We ask three questions. (1) Can the earthquake signal that near-fault studies describe survive the standardisation steps that any network analysis must apply? (2) Do the Japanese ITRDB sites show any growth response to the earthquakes that a ground-motion model says they should have felt? (3) If not, how large a response is excluded, and what would a tree-ring array need to look like to detect a far-field signal? To answer these questions without tuning the analysis to the outcome, we declared decision rules in advance at each stage and recorded them with the results (Section 2.8); the decision to turn the project from a reconstruction into a detection-limit study was one of those rules.

## 2. Data and methods

### 2.1 Tree-ring data

We used every Japanese ring-width measurement file in the ITRDB (NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information, accessed 2026-09-04; Table 1): five *Picea glehnii* sites in Hokkaido (Mount Asahidake, Teshio, Moshiri, Tokachi, Onnebetsu) and two more Hokkaido *Picea* sites (Mount Onnebetsu, Shiretoko), one *Tsuga diversifolia* site at 2,200 m in central Honshu (Senjo, Nagano, near the Itoigawa–Shizuoka Tectonic Line), one *Chamaecyparis obtusa* site in Shikoku (Omogo, Ehime, near the Median Tectonic Line), three *Cryptomeria japonica* collections on Yakushima, and one short *Pinus densiflora* collection at Miyajima (Hiroshima Bay). The data were contributed to the ITRDB by Davi et al. (2006, 2011), Jacoby et al. (2011), Yasue (2013a–g), Sano (2013), Kimura (2013) and Kaiser (2002), and each collection is cited with its dataset DOI in the reference list; the global representativity of the ITRDB, and its bias towards climate-sensitive sites, is discussed by Zhao et al. (2019). In total 650 series and 167,542 annual ring widths span 1–2005 CE. Eight of the sites also have maximum latewood density (MXD) measurements (79,058 values), which we used as an independent proxy. Site coordinates, species, and investigators were read from the NOAA template headers; the data are cited individually in the Data availability statement.

The three Yakushima collections (japa017, japa018, japa020) lie within 15 km of each other and were merged into one site (“Yakushima merged”, 181 series) for all site-level analyses, while the individual collections were retained for comparison. The merged record has at least 10 living trees in every year from 644 to 2005 CE and therefore covers all nine documented great Nankai Trough earthquakes (684 to 1946).

### 2.2 Earthquake catalogue and source geometry

Instrumental events were taken through the FDSN event service (M ≥ 5.0, 1900–2026, 128–146.5°E, 30–46°N; 5,834 events with origin time, depth and magnitude). Instrumental events were obtained from the USGS ComCat catalogue (U.S. Geological Survey, 2026). Pre-instrumental events (22 events, 684–1894 CE, M ≥ 7.0) were taken from the standard Japanese compilation (Usami et al., 2013) with the textbook epicentres and magnitudes; their locations are uncertain at the ~0.5° level, which is immaterial at the resolution used here.

The instrumental catalogue is complete at M ≥ 5 for the whole period at the resolution used here, and the M ≥ 7.5 events that matter for the array—six since 1931 (Table 2)—are among the best-located earthquakes in Japan. The historical events are used only for context and for the exclusion windows of the injection tests; no bound in this paper rests on a historical location.

Because far-field shaking depends on the closest distance to the rupture rather than on the epicentre, events with M ≥ 6.5 were assigned to approximate rectangular source areas for the Nankai Trough (Hyuga-nada and the segments A–E of Ando, 1975), the Sagami Trough, the Japan Trench (three segments), the Kuril Trench (Tokachi-oki, Nemuro-oki, Shikotan-oki, Etorofu-oki), the Hidaka zone, and eight inland fault zones. An event was assigned to the rectangle that contains its epicentre or, failing that, to the nearest rectangle within 60 km; events deeper than 60 km were treated as intraslab. The rectangles are deliberately simple; their effect on the results is tested in Section 3.7 by expanding and shrinking every rectangle by up to 50 km.

### 2.3 Ground-motion prior

For each site–event pair we computed the peak ground acceleration expected at the site from the attenuation relation of Si and Midorikawa (1999), which is the relation used operationally by the Japan Meteorological Agency and includes a fault-type term (crustal, interplate, intraslab). The distance argument was the closest distance to the assigned rectangle (with depth added in quadrature) for shallow events and the hypocentral distance for unassigned or intraslab events. The PGA was converted to a prior probability that trees respond by a logistic function in log₁₀ PGA with its midpoint at 100 gal and a slope such that the probability is ~0.1 at 40 gal and ~0.9 at 250 gal. This prior was declared as a placeholder to be re-fitted to the data (Section 2.6); it served to select, before looking at the trees, which site–event pairs *should* show a response. Eighty-one pairs had a prior probability ≥ 0.5 inside the years for which a site is usable (76 with complete SEA windows); they involve the 1952 (M8.1), 1968 (M7.9) and 2003 (M8.2) Tokachi-oki, 1973 Nemuro-oki (M7.7), and 1993 Kushiro-oki (M7.6, intraslab) earthquakes at the Hokkaido sites; the 1931 (M7.9) and 1941 (M8.0) Hyuga-nada earthquakes at the Yakushima and Omogo sites; and the 1703, 1854, 1891, 1923 and 1964 earthquakes at Senjo (Fig. 1).

### 2.4 Standardisation and the injection–recovery test

Ring-width series were detrended with five methods spanning the conservative-to-flexible range: a modified negative exponential (Fritts, 1976); smoothing splines with 50 % frequency response at 100 years and at two-thirds of the series length (Cook and Peters, 1981), implemented as a second-difference penalised smoother whose smoothing parameter gives exactly the specified frequency response; regional curve standardisation (RCS) with one age curve per species group; and signal-free RCS (Melvin and Briffa, 2008). Indices are ratios to the fitted curve and site chronologies are Tukey biweight means (Cook and Kairiukstis, 1990). Because the ITRDB files carry no pith-offset information, cambial age for RCS was approximated by ring count from the first measured ring. Running rbar and the expressed population signal (EPS; Wigley et al., 1984) were computed in 50-year windows.

Any detrending removes low-frequency variance and may remove a multi-year earthquake suppression with it. Rather than assume that it does not, we measured it. For every site and method, an earthquake-shaped suppression—a multiplicative growth loss of amplitude *A* in the event year, decaying as 1.0, 0.6, 0.35, 0.2, 0.1 over five years—was injected into a random fraction *f* of the living trees at 25 random years located at least 10 years from any catalogued M ≥ 7 event, for *A* = 0.20, 0.35, 0.50. The site was re-standardised and the paired difference between the clean and injected chronologies was matched against the injected profile. Three quantities follow: *preservation*, the recovered amplitude on the arithmetic-mean chronology divided by *A·f* (1.0 means detrending removed nothing); *leakage*, the mean absolute distortion outside the five-year window; and the signal-to-noise ratio, the recovered amplitude on the biweight chronology divided by the matched-filter noise of the clean chronology. The selection rule, declared before the real data were run, rejected any method with median preservation below 0.70 at *A* = 0.35 and chose the surviving method with the highest median signal-to-noise ratio.

Two “usable year” gates were carried forward: a climate-quality gate (EPS ≥ 0.85, depth ≥ 5) and, for event detection, a depth gate of at least 10 living trees. The distinction matters for Yakushima, where between-tree correlation is low (as is typical for *Cryptomeria* in a humid subtropical climate) and the EPS gate would discard most of the record even though the number of living trees is large.

### 2.5 Detection statistic

The site-level statistic follows the growth-change convention of disturbance dendroecology (Nowacki and Abrams, 1997): gc(*t*) = mean(index, *t*..*t*+1) / mean(index, *t*−5..*t*−1) − 1. For each site the distribution of gc over usable years gives a mean and standard deviation, and the response of a site to an event is *z* = −(gc − mean)/sd, positive for suppression. A pair is “detected” at *z* ≥ 1.645 (one-sided 95 %). At the tree level, the responding fraction *f* is the fraction of living trees whose own gc falls below their personal 10th percentile.

The event year was the growing-season effective year: an earthquake in August or later cannot alter a ring that is already formed, so its first affected ring is that of the following year (a rule noted empirically for the 1907 and 1923 Pamir earthquakes by Owczarek et al., 2017, and for the 1960 Jiuzhaigou-area earthquake whose signal appears in the 1961 ring by Fan et al., 2020). This affects, among others, the November 1941 Hyuga-nada, October 1707 Hoei and December 1854 Ansei earthquakes. Calendar-year timing is reported as an ablation.

### 2.6 Confronting the prior with the trees

For all 3,193 site–event pairs with M ≥ 6.5 inside a site's usable span we computed *z* and *f*, fitted a logistic regression of detection on log₁₀ PGA by bounded maximum likelihood, and computed the area under the receiver-operating curve (AUC) of the prior as a predictor of detection. Superposed epoch analysis was run per site on the events with prior probability ≥ 0.5, from five years before to ten years after the event, against 2,000 composites of the same number of random years.

### 2.7 Detection limits, upper bounds and array design

Using the injection machinery with the responding fraction measured in Section 2.6, we injected amplitudes *A* = 0.05–0.50 at 30 random usable years per site and recomputed *z*, which gives the single-site detection power P(detect | *A*) and the sensitivity slope d*z*/d*A*. Under the null the statistic is standard normal by construction and an injected amplitude shifts it by slope × *A*, so the one-sided 95 % upper bound on the amplitude consistent with an observed *z* is *A*₉₅ = (*z* + 1.645)/slope. Events seen by several sites receive an array bound from the stacked statistic *z*ₐ = Σ*z*ᵢ/√*S* with slope Σslopeᵢ/√*S*. Because the slope is proportional to *f*, the quantity *A·f*—the stand-mean growth loss—is independent of the assumed responding fraction, and we report bounds in that unit. Fitting the dependence of the slope on living-tree depth *n* gives the number of coherent sites *S* needed to detect an amplitude *A* at 3σ from *z* = slope(*n*) · *A* · √*S*.

### 2.8 Replication of published criteria with random-year nulls

We applied the family of criteria used in the network-scale literature to the same pairs and, in each case, to random years. Tree-level “responsive” criteria: ring width in the event year or the next below 90, 80, 70 or 60 % of the mean of the preceding three years; the abrupt-growth-change criterion of Schweingruber et al. (1990) at 25 % and 40 %; and the pointer index of Cropper (1979) below −0.75. Site-level statistics: the double-bootstrap SEA of Rao et al. (2019) pooled over all prior-selected pairs, by distance bin and by trench; a lag-tolerant statistic (the most negative gc over lags 0–5, generous to the “1–5 years after” claim); and the resistance, recovery and resilience indices of Lloret et al. (2011). Each statistic was compared with (i) random years drawn independently per site—the design of the published tests—and (ii) a year-shift null in which one common offset δ (8 ≤ |δ| ≤ 250 years) is added to all event years, which preserves the spatial co-occurrence of events and the between-site climate correlation. A single primary test was pre-specified before the exploratory family was run: the prior-weighted stack of pair *z*-scores over all pairs with prior probability ≥ 0.2, tested against the year-shift null; all other tests (45) are reported as exploratory with the total number of tests and the corresponding Bonferroni threshold.

### 2.9 Pre-registration record

Decision rules were written into the project record before the corresponding result was seen: the standardisation selection rule (R1), the depth-based usable-year gate (R2), the placeholder status of the ground-motion prior (R3), the rule that the project becomes a detection-limit study if the prior has no skill and the Hokkaido SEA is non-significant (R4), the form of the upper bound (R5), and the growing-season effective year (R6). The record, generated automatically with timestamps and the evidence files for each rule, is provided as Supplementary Text S1. All analyses were run on a synthetic testbed with embedded suppressions before the real data, and every detection statistic reported here recovers the embedded signals there (Supplementary Text S2).

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Chronologies and usable years

The thirteen sites yield chronologies of 107 (Miyajima) to 2,005 (Yakushima) years (Table 1; Figs. S1–S3). Under the climate-quality gate (EPS ≥ 0.85 and ≥ 5 trees) the Hokkaido chronologies become usable between 1517 (Tokachi) and 1720 (Shiretoko, Mount Onnebetsu), Senjo in 1728, Omogo in 1760, and the merged Yakushima record from 976 CE, though with gaps before 1500; the Miyajima collection never reaches the threshold and was excluded. Under the depth gate used for event detection (≥ 10 living trees) the usable spans are longer and, importantly, continuous: Tokachi from 1567, Onnebetsu from 1592, Senjo from 1646, Mount Asahidake from 1669, Teshio from 1704, Omogo from 1717, Shiretoko and Mount Onnebetsu from 1749, Moshiri from 1775, and the merged Yakushima record from 644 CE without interruption (1,362 years; the japa020 collection alone reaches the depth gate from 644 to 1809). The two gates disagree most at Yakushima, where the running rbar of *Cryptomeria* is low (EPS mostly 0.6–0.8 before 1500) although the number of living trees exceeds 20 for most of the last millennium; this is the situation in which a climate-quality criterion would discard the very record that a detection study needs, and it is the reason the two gates are kept separate.

For the instrumental period the array is therefore complete: every Hokkaido site is usable throughout 1900–1997 (2003 at Shiretoko and Mount Onnebetsu), and the southern sites throughout 1900–2001. The events the prior selects fall inside these spans, with the exception of the 2003 Tokachi-oki earthquake, which post-dates the end of four Hokkaido collections and is seen by two sites only.

### 3.2 Standardisation preserves the target signal

All five detrending methods preserved the injected five-year suppression almost entirely (median preservation at *A* = 0.35: signal-free RCS 1.02, RCS 1.00, negative exponential 0.98, flexible spline 0.96, 100-year spline 0.93; Fig. 2a). The concern that standardisation erases earthquake signals is therefore not borne out for a signal of this shape, even for the flexible spline, which is effectively rigid for series several centuries long. The methods differed instead in the noise they left behind: the two RCS variants, which cannot be constrained without pith offsets, left twice the matched-filter noise of the splines (signal-to-noise ratio 0.50–0.53 versus 1.04–1.08) and in some sites increased rather than reduced the variance of the site mean. By the pre-declared rule the 100-year spline was selected; the runner-up methods are carried through the ablations in Section 3.7.

The most consequential number from the injection test is the single-site signal-to-noise ratio itself: a 35 % growth loss in 60 % of the trees produces a chronology anomaly of about one standard deviation. Single chronologies of this kind cannot detect an M8 earthquake; any detection must come from the coherence of several sites.

### 3.3 No relation between predicted shaking and observed growth

Among the 3,193 site–event pairs, 194 (6.1 %) exceeded the detection threshold, against the 5.0 % expected from a one-sided 95 % test if no pair responded—an excess of about one percentage point, which is the same weak tendency that the pooled tests of Section 3.6 quantify. The logistic fit of detection on log₁₀ PGA ran to its bound (midpoint at 10^4.5 gal) and the AUC was 0.46 (Fig. 3): the ground-motion prior, which correctly ranks the 1952 Tokachi-oki earthquake at the Hokkaido sites and the 1941 Hyuga-nada earthquake at Yakushima as the strongest expected responses, had no skill in predicting where growth suppressions occurred. The tree-level responding fraction at detected pairs (median 0.47) was indistinguishable from that at undetected pairs. The strongest detections illustrate what the threshold picks up: the largest *z* (3.06) is a single anomalous year, 1948, at Teshio in northern Hokkaido, which is matched by the pairing rule to seven M 6.5–7.4 earthquakes in that year from Hyuga-nada to Nobi, all of them more than 800 km away and all with prior PGA of a few gal or less. Such coincidences are exactly what an uncalibrated search would report as responses.

Superposed epoch analyses on the prior-selected events were non-significant at all twelve sites (one-sided *p* ≥ 0.15, minimum at Shiretoko; Fig. 4). The composite for the five Hokkaido sites that should have felt the 1952 Tokachi-oki earthquake (six with the merged record), and for the four southern sites that should have felt the 1931 and 1941 Hyuga-nada earthquakes, lies within the 5–95 % band of random-year composites throughout the −5 to +10 year window. Maximum latewood density gave the same answer (SEA *p* ≥ 0.09 at nine sites; AUC 0.54).

### 3.4 Sensitivity, power and upper bounds

Injection at the measured responding fraction (*f* = 0.47) gave single-site sensitivities of 1.8 (Senjo) to 3.2 (Moshiri) *z*-units per unit amplitude (Fig. 5a). No site reached 95 % detection power even at *A* = 0.50, i.e. a halving of growth in the event year in half of the trees. Sensitivity increased only as *n*^0.29 with living-tree depth—much less than the √*n* expected for independent trees—showing that the noise is dominated by between-tree common variance, i.e. climate.

Stacking sites converts non-detection into a bound (Fig. 6; Table 2; sites and their usable spans are listed in Table 1). For the 1952 Tokachi-oki earthquake (M8.1, six sites, mean prior PGA ≈ 190 gal) the stacked statistic was *z*ₐ = −0.55 and the 95 % upper bound on stand-mean growth loss in the event year is 7.1 %; for the 1941 Hyuga-nada earthquake (M8.0, four sites, ≈150 gal) it is 9.3 %; for the intraslab 1993 Kushiro-oki earthquake (M7.6, six sites) 2.8 %. Bounds for the M7.5–7.9 events are 15–26 %. Individual pairs with prior PGA ≥ 100 gal (n = 94) have a median single-site bound of 0.61 in amplitude units, illustrating again that only the array constrains anything.

### 3.5 Published criteria applied to random years

The tree-level “responsive” criteria classify large fractions of trees as responsive in years without earthquakes (Fig. 7): with a 10 % reduction criterion, the median fraction at random years is 0.51 and the 95th percentile of the mean over a site's event years is 0.67; with the Schweingruber 25 % criterion the random-year median is 0.13. Event-year fractions exceeded the random-year median for 10 of 14 criterion–series combinations (e.g. 0.14 versus 0.09 for a 40 % reduction), but no site exceeded the 95th percentile of the appropriately sized null for more than two criteria, and site-level one-sided *p*-values had medians of 0.19–0.54. The 64–77 % responsive fractions reported for the Himalayas (Pandey et al., 2023) are within the range that a 10–20 % reduction criterion produces at random years in the Japanese data.

The pooled double-bootstrap SEA (Rao et al., 2019) over the 76 prior-selected pairs was non-significant (years 0–1: *p* = 0.20; minimum over lags 0–5: *p* = 0.25), as were the raw-width version, all distance bins (100–200 km: *p* = 0.10, n = 26) and all trenches. The lag-tolerant statistic did not differ between events and random years (Mann–Whitney *p* = 0.57). The one exploratory statistic that reached nominal significance was the Lloret resistance index: median 0.975 at event years versus 1.002 at random years, one-sided *p* = 0.005 against independent random years.

### 3.6 Nulls that preserve the spatial structure of events

Against the year-shift null, which keeps the fact that six sites share the 1952 event and that Hokkaido sites share their climate, the resistance result weakened to *p* = 0.030, the responsive-fraction excesses to *p* = 0.10–0.60, and the SEA to *p* = 0.23–0.27 (Table 3). With 47 tests reported the Bonferroni threshold is 0.0011 and no exploratory test survives it; a Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995) at a false-discovery rate of 0.05 selects none either. The pre-specified primary test—the prior-weighted stack over all 167 pairs with prior probability ≥ 0.2—gave *z*_w = 1.58 against a year-shift null with median −0.07 and 5–95 % range −1.89 to 1.83 (*p* = 0.074), i.e. a tendency towards suppression that does not reach significance, and a pooled 95 % upper bound on stand-mean growth loss of 4.7 % (amplitude 0.10 at *f* = 0.47) over all pairs the prior considers relevant. The resistance result varied between *p* = 0.03 and 0.05 across null seeds (Table 3). The picture is therefore a weak, consistent tendency towards lower growth in event years of the order of 2–3 % of stand mean, which is compatible with the upper bounds of Section 3.4 and which the appropriate null does not distinguish from zero.

### 3.7 Robustness

The array bounds changed by at most 0.03 in stand-mean units across nine alternative analyses (Fig. S6): calendar-year instead of effective-year timing (which lowers the 1941 bound to 4.9 % because the November event is then matched to a ring it could not have affected), PGA-weighted instead of equal-weight stacking, source rectangles expanded or shrunk by 25 and 50 km, and the negative-exponential and flexible-spline standardisations run through the whole chain from per-tree indices to injection slopes. The 1952 bound moved from 7.1 % to 2.7 % when rectangles were expanded by 50 km because a seventh site entered the stack with a negative *z*; no variant loosened the bound of any M ≥ 8 event beyond 12 %.

### 3.8 What an array would need

From the fitted sensitivity scaling, detecting a stand-mean growth loss of 10 % at 3σ would require 141 coherent sites of 30 trees, and a 5 % loss 564 sites (Fig. 8; Table 4). A 30 % loss—the order of magnitude documented at near-fault trees—would require 16 such sites, and a 50 % loss a handful. Increasing trees per site helps slowly (*n*^0.29); reducing the distance to the rupture, where the amplitude is large, is the only efficient design.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Far-field insensitivity and its physical reading

The Japanese ITRDB sites experienced, by a standard attenuation relation, peak accelerations of 100–250 gal from six M ≥ 7.5 earthquakes since 1931 without a detectable change in ring width or maximum density. The bounds—less than 7 % stand-mean growth loss for an M8.1 at 100–200 km—are an order of magnitude below the suppressions that near-fault trees show, and they are consistent with the strong-motion evidence that trees are damaged only by very strong shaking (Hough, 2026) and with the near-fault character of every convincing dendroseismic record, from the San Andreas (Jacoby et al., 1988; Carroll et al., 2025) to the North Anatolian fault (Kozacı, 2012). The result is not surprising to a seismologist. Its value is that it is quantitative, that it was obtained with the same data sources and statistical tools as the network-scale studies that report far-field signals, and that it was obtained without the freedom to choose the criterion after seeing the outcome.

The bounds can be placed against the thresholds reported by studies that did find responses. At Jiuzhaigou on the eastern Tibetan Plateau, spruce growth was suppressed for 3–9 years by five earthquakes over 350 years, and isoseismal modelling put the threshold for a significant effect at MMI 6.2–6.8, corresponding to Mw ≥ 5.3 within 20 km or Mw ≥ 7.4 within 115 km (Fan et al., 2020). The Japanese sites lie at 100–250 km from the M8 ruptures, and the prior PGA of 150–200 gal at the nearest of them is at the lower edge of that intensity range; the absence of a response at ≤ 7–9 % is therefore consistent with the Jiuzhaigou threshold rather than in conflict with it, provided the threshold is read as a lower limit for a detectable stand-scale effect. The Pamir juniper record, in which suppressions were attributed to earthquakes up to 230 km away (Owczarek et al., 2017), and the Himalayan network, in which two-thirds of trees were counted as responsive within 200 km (Pandey et al., 2023), sit on the other side of that boundary; the difference is unlikely to be one of distance alone. Junipers on rock slopes in a semi-arid range, or trees on Himalayan hillslopes prone to coseismic landsliding, may well respond through geomorphic mechanisms that a stable, mesic Japanese *Picea* or *Cryptomeria* stand does not experience—but if so, the response is a property of the site, not of the shaking, and cannot be transferred to a network without a site-by-site calibration of the kind performed here.

Two properties of climate-oriented tree-ring sites make them poor seismometers even before attenuation is considered. They are chosen for climate sensitivity and for stand stability—ridges, upper tree lines, stable slopes—so the geomorphic mechanisms that transmit shaking to growth (root shearing, ground cracking, landslides, changed water tables) are precisely the processes that site selection avoids. And the between-tree common variance that makes them good climate recorders is what limits the detectability of a small stand-wide anomaly: the sensitivity we measured grows only as *n*^0.29, so replication cannot substitute for proximity.

### 4.2 Why network-scale studies can report signals that are not there

The replication exercise identifies two mechanisms. The first is the criterion. A tree is called responsive when its ring is some percentage narrower than the preceding years; because ring width is a noisy, autocorrelated series, such criteria fire in a large fraction of trees in every year (Fig. 7), and the fraction at event years is only modestly above that background. Reporting the event-year fraction alone, as is common, presents the background as signal. The second is the null. Independent random-year nulls, including the double-bootstrap SEA of Rao et al. (2019) in the way it has been applied to earthquakes, treat *S* sites that share an event year as *S* independent observations; the problem is the one identified for solar–weather superposed epoch studies four decades ago (Haurwitz and Brier, 1981), and its remedy—a null that preserves the correlation structure of the data—is the same. When those sites also share climate—as any regional network does—the variance of the pooled statistic is underestimated and *p*-values are too small. In our data a resistance decrease of 2.5 % had *p* = 0.005 against the independent null and *p* = 0.03 against the year-shift null; it was one of 46 tests. Gao et al. (2024) reported resilience shifts at sites with MMI ≥ 4, i.e. at accelerations of a few tens of gal, well below the range in which we could exclude even a 7 % effect at 190 gal; the analysis here does not test their specific regions, but it shows that their evidential design would have supported a positive claim in Japan had one been sought.

A second escape route from the null result is that earthquakes act on trees hydrologically—through altered groundwater, spring discharge or soil moisture—rather than mechanically, so that ring width is the wrong variable. Wood-anatomical and carbon-isotope responses of this kind have been documented near the epicentre of the 2010 Maule earthquake (Mohr et al., 2021). Maximum latewood density is the proxy most sensitive to growing-season water and temperature status among those available in the ITRDB, and it responds to a change in late-season water supply within the same year. At the eight Japanese sites with density series, the density chronologies were as silent as the width chronologies: the superposed epoch composites were non-significant everywhere (*p* ≥ 0.09), the prior had no skill (AUC 0.54), and the width–density correlation of 0.19–0.46 shows that density carries information that width does not (Fig. S7). The hydrological route is therefore not merely unresolved by our width analysis; at these sites it is tested and, at the resolution of an annual proxy, closed. Isotopic proxies remain untested because none exist for the Japanese ITRDB sites.

We do not conclude that far-field growth responses are absent everywhere. Ecohydrological responses to earthquakes are documented at the wood-anatomical level (Mohr et al., 2021) and local site factors modulate them (Fu et al., 2020). We conclude that in a network of ordinary tree-ring sites they are small—compatible with a few percent of stand mean—and that establishing them requires calibrated detection statistics and spatially structured nulls. The injection–recovery test and the year-shift null are simple to add to any SEA-based study, and we provide the code.

### 4.3 Consequences for dendroseismic reconstruction

The original aim of this project was to reconstruct the multi-century clustering of great earthquakes—the sequence of Nankai Trough ruptures from 684 to 1946 in particular—from the Yakushima record, which has ≥ 10 living trees in every year since 644 CE. That aim cannot be met with this record: the physical prior gives Yakushima a probability below 0.2 of responding even to the M8.6 Hoei earthquake of 1707, and the calibrated bounds show why. The record is nonetheless a remarkable archive, and its value for the Hyuga-nada and southwestern Japan events (1931, 1941) would be tested directly by isotopic or anatomical proxies that respond to hydrological change rather than to mechanical damage.

For reconstruction, the design numbers of Section 3.8 point unambiguously to near-fault sampling, and they are specific enough to plan with (Table 4). The number of coherent sites needed to detect a stand-level amplitude *A* at 3σ falls from 564 at *A* = 0.05 to 141 at 0.10, 63 at 0.15, 36 at 0.20 and 16 at 0.30 for sites of 30 trees, and the weak dependence on tree number (sensitivity ∝ *n*^0.29) means that doubling the trees per site buys only a 20 % gain in sensitivity. Distance to the rupture, which sets *A*, is the only lever with leverage. Near-fault studies report amplitudes of order 0.3 to 1 in trees within a few hundred metres of the trace—the 1943 Tosya rupture suppressed 14 of 28 sampled pines (Kozacı, 2012), and coast redwoods on the San Andreas show suppressions of years' duration (Carroll et al., 2025)—so at such amplitudes a dozen or fewer stands of ordinary size are sufficient, and the array-scale tests developed here become decisive rather than marginal.

Three properties of an efficient dendroseismic array follow. First, geometry: short transects perpendicular to the trace (stands at roughly 0, 1, 3 and 10 km) repeated at several points along strike, so that the along-strike coherence and the across-strike decay that distinguish a rupture from a regional climate anomaly are both sampled; this is the spatial signature that a network model can learn, and the counterpart of the array-design problem solved for distributed acoustic sensing networks. Second, calibration: an instrumentally recorded rupture on the same fault, so that the responding fraction, the amplitude–distance relation and the effective-year timing can be measured rather than assumed—the 2016 Futagawa (Kumamoto) and 2024 Noto ruptures, both through forested terrain, and the well-mapped traces of the Itoigawa–Shizuoka Tectonic Line and the Median Tectonic Line, are natural candidates in Japan. Third, analysis: the injection–recovery calibration, the effective-year rule, the array stacking and the year-shift null transfer to such an array without modification, and the pre-registration record (Text S1) provides the template for declaring the detection rules before the cores are measured. With an array of this kind, the multi-century record that the Yakushima trees could not deliver—the sequence and clustering of surface-rupturing earthquakes on inland faults over the lifetime of the oldest trees—is within reach.

### 4.4 Limitations

The source rectangles are approximate, although their effect on the bounds is small. The attenuation relation predicts PGA on soil; sites on rock would have felt less, strengthening the conclusion. The responding-fraction assumption enters only the amplitude unit, not the stand-mean unit in which the main results are stated. The year-shift null preserves spatial structure but assumes stationarity of the chronology statistics over the offset range; the exploratory tests are numerous and are labelled as such. The Yakushima record illustrates a further point: it has by far the most living trees in any year but only average sensitivity (2.0–2.7 *z* per unit amplitude), because the between-tree correlation of *Cryptomeria* in a humid subtropical climate is low; tree number cannot substitute for the common signal that a stand-wide disturbance must overcome, which is why we gate usable years by depth for detection but report sensitivity site by site. Finally, MXD was available at eight sites only, and no isotopic proxies exist for the Japanese ITRDB sites.

## 5. Conclusions

Calibrating a tree-ring detection statistic before using it changes what a null result means. In the Japanese ITRDB network, no growth response to earthquakes is detectable, and the calibrated bounds show that M8 subduction earthquakes at 100–250 km suppressed stand-mean growth by less than 7–9 %. The published criteria and nulls that have supported far-field earthquake signals elsewhere produce comparable “responses” at random years and overstate significance when events are shared across sites. Reconstructing earthquake histories from trees remains possible—near the fault.

Three practical recommendations follow for any study that searches tree-ring networks for earthquake signals. Calibrate the detection statistic by injection before applying it, and report the amplitude the method could have missed alongside any detection. Report every “responsive-tree” fraction together with the same fraction at random years, using a null that averages over the same number of years as the event set. And test pooled statistics against a null that keeps the co-occurrence of events across sites—a common year shift is the simplest—rather than against independently drawn years. With these three additions the published network-scale results become testable in the same way as the Japanese case, and the far-field question can be settled region by region on evidence that a sceptic and an advocate would both accept.
